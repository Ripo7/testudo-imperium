How to rule

Goal

Hold 60% of the provinces, or wipe out the three other powers. If you lose every major city, or a rival gets there first, you fall.

A province is yours only while you hold every place in it: its major city, all its towns, and any fort finished inside it. Split between holders, it is disputed (hatched on the map, each part in its holder's colour): it counts for nobody. So an enemy fort, or one town still free, keeps a province from you.

Growth has a brake: past its fair share of the continent's settlements (a major city counts three times; 30% while four powers stand, more as powers fall) an empire is overstretched (income falls about 0.8% for every point above it, and conquered cities settle more slowly), and the other powers turn on whoever holds half of the provinces.

Wars are fought for provinces: a power goes to war more readily against a rival that holds part of a province it means to finish, and makes peace less readily with it. A power close to victory won't make peace with anyone behind it, nor will the one ahead when only two powers are left.

Keeping track

  • Dispatches (top left) list what needs your attention right now: a city under assault, an enemy army closing on one of your cities, a plundered province, food or gold about to run out, a city that may revolt, a rival close to victory, a treaty about to end. Each one lasts as long as its cause. Click it to go there.
  • The standings show the provinces each power holds whole, with their change over the last minute (▲▼), a bar towards victory and its men under arms. Below them, every war and treaty, with how long it has lasted or has left.
  • Overview (O): your treasury and every army and city at a glance (Empire), who fights whom with war scores and weariness (Wars), and the chronicle of the war: wars, treaties, battles, cities taken, revolts. Under the fog it only tells what your scouts saw, and what everyone knows.
  • The eye in the top bar (V) hides the standings, dispatches, messages and hints at once, to clear the map. While they are hidden, a red number on the eye counts the urgent dispatches. The pause menu can switch each of them off for good.
  • When the war ends, a report tells how it went: provinces, armies and income over time, every power's numbers, its economy (what it earned, and spent on pay, food, recruits and buildings), each war and its outcome, and the key moments.
  • On a phone the panel opens over the bottom third of the screen: pull its handle up to see all of it, down to close it.

Marching

  • Armies march twice as fast on roads, at a normal pace on plains and steppe, slower in desert and hills, slowly through forests and very slowly over mountains, where they also lose stragglers. The high peaks can't be crossed.
  • Wide rivers can only be crossed on a bridge, where a road crosses them. Narrow streams can be waded at a ford, slowly. Select an army and point at the map to see what the ground means for it.
  • Cavalry only outpaces infantry on open ground and on roads, and siege engines crawl once they leave the roads.

Fog of war

Every power sees only the land around its cities, armies and forts; armies with cavalry, and armies on high ground, see further. Enemy armies and battles outside that are hidden, and you can't tell how well an unseen city is defended. The AI plays by the same rule: it only reacts to the armies it sees, and judges unseen cities by its last report. An army chasing an enemy that slips out of sight marches to where it was last seen, then gives up. In autoplay you watch through the eyes of the power picked in the standings. The fog can be turned off on the title screen.

War and peace

  • The war starts with a 4-minute truce: the powers race for the neutral towns and march past each other.
  • After that, powers stay at peace until one declares war: the AI picks a neighbour it thinks it can beat, usually one war at a time, and rarely while there are still free towns to take. Attacking a power you are at peace with declares war.
  • Wars wear powers down: battle losses, lost cities, hunger, unpaid troops, other fronts and simply time. A worn-out or losing power seeks peace, and pays tribute if the other side must be bought; a winning one demands tribute. The AI powers make peace with each other the same way.
  • Click a rival in the standings to talk: offer peace (free, or for the tribute they ask), renew a treaty, or declare war. AI envoys come to you when they want peace.
  • A peace treaty binds for 2½ minutes. The AI keeps its treaties. Break one and you are an oath-breaker: nobody will deal with you for 4 minutes.
  • Armies at war plunder enemy provinces they stand in (the owner loses 60% of that land's income) and live off the land (no extra food cost abroad).
  • A freshly conquered city with low loyalty, no garrison and no temple may revolt, especially in an overstretched empire.

Difficulty (chosen on the title screen): Tiro the AI earns 20% less and attacks later · Legatus an even fight · Imperator the AI earns 25% more and attacks sooner.

Economy

  • A province's land produces plains and steppe → food, forests → wood, hills and mountains → stone. That income is split between its settlements (the major city takes the biggest slice, each town a smaller one), and goes to whoever holds each. Hold every settlement of a province and it yields +12%. The major city also colours the province on the map.
  • Every city produces gold plus a specialty. Armies eat food every second, more when they campaign outside your own land, and soldiers draw pay in gold (horses and elephants count twice). If food runs out armies starve; if gold runs out soldiers desert.
  • Stone raises walls and forts and builds siege engines; wood feeds archers, siege engines and forts.
  • Market (in your major cities): buy 100 food, wood or stone for 130 gold, or sell 100 for 70 (Carthage trades at 110 and 95).

Building

  • Select one of your cities to recruit troops (they muster beside the city) or raise walls (up to level 3). Walls multiply the militia's defense.
  • Improvements (a major city has two slots, a town one): Farmlands +0.9 food/s · Forum +0.8 gold/s · Barracks recruit 30% faster and +70 militia · Temple loyalty returns 3× faster and half the overstretch penalty. An enemy who takes the city loots the last one.
  • Forts are built in a province where you hold at least one place. Once finished, a fort is one more place to hold: an enemy must storm it to win the province, and a fort of yours in a rival's province keeps it disputed. Forts also block the road (enemies at war must storm them) and guard the countryside: enemy armies within 6 squares of a fort cannot plunder.

Battles: automatic, but decided by strategy

  • Retreat: once the lines have met, an attacking or field army can pull out. It saves what is left, minus 6–40% to the pursuit (more if the enemy has cavalry, siege engines are half lost). Defenders cannot leave their walls.
  • Counters: spearmen ×1.9 vs cavalry · cavalry ×2 vs archers and siege, and ×1.8 on the charge · archers fire three volleys before the lines meet.
  • An army waiting in forest, hills or mountains defends better; attacking across a river is a −20% penalty; attacking from two sides is a +25% bonus.
  • Stance: Aggressive hits harder; Testudo (a defensive stance for the other powers) defends better and makes legionaries nearly immune to arrows.
  • Every 2 siege engines remove one wall level during an assault. Cavalry is weak in assaults and rough ground.
  • Armies that lose rout home; cavalry on the winning side cuts them down as they flee. Winning battles earns veteran stars.
  • Select your army, then point at a target: you'll see the odds before you commit.

The four powers

    Autoplay

    From the title screen (or the pause menu, mid-campaign) you can let the AI run all four powers and just watch. The camera follows the biggest battles and conquests; drag or zoom to take it over, press Follow (F) to give it back. Click a power in the standings to see its treasury, click any army to follow it.

    Saving

    Your campaign is saved in this browser whenever you leave the page, open the menu or return to the title screen, and every few seconds while you play. Press Continue on the title screen to pick it up where you left off. A finished campaign is not kept.

    Reading the map

    Each power's land is tinted in its colour, with a stronger glow along its borders; free land is greyed out. A disputed province is hatched, each part tinted for whoever holds the nearest place, and its name is underlined with dashes. Zoom out and the colours get stronger, or switch to the Political map (P) for flat colours.
